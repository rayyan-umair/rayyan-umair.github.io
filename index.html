01 / About

The person people call
when no one
knows why.

I have always been the person people call when something breaks and no one knows why. Not because I always have the answer immediately. But because I do not leave until there is one.

That quality — the refusal to walk away from an unresolved problem — is what pulled me into cybersecurity. And it is what is now pulling me further. Past the helpdesk. Past the tools. Into the harder question underneath all of it.

Who is responsible when the system fails?

We are building a world where AI monitors the network, triages the alerts, and patches the vulnerabilities. Faster than any analyst. More consistently than any team. And I think that is genuinely good. I am not afraid of it.

But I have noticed something. The more we automate the execution, the more exposed the judgment layer becomes. Someone still has to decide what the AI protects. Someone still has to own the call when the model is wrong. Someone still has to walk into the boardroom and explain what happened — and why it will not happen again.

That person cannot just be technical. They have to be trustworthy.

I am building toward that role. Not rushing toward it. Building toward it — with the technical foundation, the strategic thinking, and the kind of calm that only comes from having actually fixed things when they were broken.
